Your liver plays a key role in your health. It has hundreds of important jobs. When too much fat begins to build up (steatosis), it affects how well your liver works. Some types of steatotic (fatty) liver disease are mild, while others can become more serious. Early care can help prevent long-term damage.

Steatotic liver disease (SLD), formerly called fatty liver disease, happens when too much fat builds up in your liver. It’s one of the most common types of liver disease. Many people don’t know they have it at first. It often doesn’t cause symptoms in the early stages.

Over time, extra fat in your liver can lead to inflammation and damage. In some people, this damage gets worse and causes scarring. Severe scarring can affect how well your liver works and lead to serious health problems.

Many conditions cause steatosis of the liver. Steatosis is often reversible, especially in the early stages and before cirrhosis develops. Treatment often focuses on managing underlying conditions. Your healthcare provider may also recommend making changes to your daily habits.

In some cases, providers may recommend medication to help manage certain health conditions. Severe cases may require a liver transplant.

Recovery depends on how early your provider finds and treats the condition. Early-stage steatotic liver disease can sometimes improve within months. More advanced disease may require long-term management.

Many people with steatotic liver disease live long, healthy lives. In some cases, making changes in your daily life can reduce fat in your liver and prevent further damage. When caught early, you can often reverse or manage the disease.

Without treatment, the disease can lead to severe scarring or cirrhosis and liver failure. But treatment can still help manage symptoms and slow progression.

Taking steps to improve your overall health can make a meaningful difference over time.

Steatotic (fatty) liver disease is common. But it doesn’t always lead to serious problems. When you get an early diagnosis, you may be able to reverse it with healthy changes. As it progresses, it can become more serious and require ongoing care. Paying attention to risk factors and symptoms can help you act sooner. And working with your provider can help protect your liver over time.
